\section{The International House Price Database}
\label{sec:database}

\subsection{Overview and Relationship to Working Paper 99}

The International House Price Database is constructed and maintained by the
Globalization Institute of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas. It provides
quarterly, seasonally adjusted, cross-country comparable indices of house
prices and personal disposable income, beginning in 1975\,Q1.

The authoritative description of the database is \citet{mack2011}, Dallas Fed
Globalization Institute Working Paper 99, which is revised whenever the
construction methodology changes. That paper documents the selection criteria
applied to national sources, the full methodological decision tree, the
treatment of each country individually, and the underlying national sources.
This section summarises the parts of that methodology that a user needs in
order to interpret the series correctly --- and, in
Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}, what follows from it. For anything beyond
that, and in particular for country-level detail, the reader should consult
Working Paper 99 directly.

The current release covers 26 economies, listed in

release quarter, with no interior gaps; how that completeness is achieved,
and what it implies, is discussed in

\paragraph{Aggregates.}
Two aggregate series are published alongside the country series. The first,
\textit{Aggregate --- 2005 Fixed Weights}, weights countries by their
purchasing-power-parity adjusted GDP shares in 2005, held constant over the
whole sample. Fixing the weights means that movements in the aggregate
reflect movements in the underlying country series rather than changes in
relative economic size or exchange rates. The second, \textit{Aggregate ---
Dynamic Weights}, allows the weights to vary over time, which tracks the
changing composition of the sample more closely at the cost of mixing price
dynamics with reweighting. The Observatory's headline charts use the
dynamically weighted aggregate. On the role of aggregation in exuberance
testing specifically, see \citet{pavlidis2018}.

\subsection{Variables}

Each economy is represented by four quarterly series, defined in

means the database supports comparison of \emph{growth rates and dynamics}
across countries, but not comparison of price \emph{levels}.

\noindent Expressing income per working-age capita, rather than per head of
total population, controls for cross-country and over-time variation in
demographic structure. The ratio RHPI/RPDI --- real prices relative to real
income per capita --- is the affordability measure used throughout the
Observatory's outputs, and is one of the two series to which the exuberance
tests of Section~\ref{sec:methodology} are applied.

\subsection{Construction Methodology}
\label{subsec:construction}

The construction of each country series follows a fixed sequence, described
in full in \citet{mack2011}. The steps below are the ones that bear on
interpretation.

\paragraph{Benchmark and source selection.}
The U.S.\ FHFA house price index serves as the benchmark against which
candidate series for other countries are judged. Preference is given, in
order, to nationwide geographic coverage, existing rather than newly built
dwellings, single-family dwellings, pricing per dwelling, availability back
to 1975, and quarterly frequency. Sources are drawn primarily from central
banks, national statistical offices and other bodies not party to housing
transactions, in preference to market participants whose samples may be
biased by their own commercial footprint. The corresponding benchmark for
income is the disposable personal income series of the U.S.\ Bureau of
Economic Analysis, with national accounts aggregates reconciled to that
concept where necessary.

\paragraph{Frequency conversion.}
Series reported more frequently than quarterly are converted by simple
averaging of the constituent months. Series reported less frequently
(semi-annual, annual, bi-annual) are interpolated up to quarterly frequency,
using a quadratic-match method. Where a country has experienced severe
inflation within the sample --- Croatia is the example given by
\citet{mack2011} --- interpolation is performed on the logged series to avoid
a fitted curve that violates non-negativity.

\paragraph{Splicing.}
Few national reference series extend back to 1975. The current reference
series for a country is therefore extended backwards using the growth rates
of older historical series, producing what \citet{mack2011} term the extended
or full-length series. Splicing uses exact growth rates, except for countries
with severe inflation episodes, where log-differences are used.

\paragraph{Backcasting and nowcasting.}
Where splicing still does not reach 1975\,Q1, the remaining observations ---
never more than about two years --- are backcast. Where the current series
lags, because a national source reports late or at lower than quarterly
frequency, the most recent observations are nowcast and replaced with actual
data in later releases. Both are produced from the same basic structural time
series model described below. The release file's \texttt{Note} sheet records
which series contain forecast values in the current vintage.

\paragraph{Seasonal adjustment.}
Seasonal adjustment is performed by the Dallas Fed using a
basic structural time series (BSTS) model in the sense of
\citet{harvey1989}, fitted by maximum likelihood with the Kalman filter and
selected by information criteria. The model decomposes each series
additively --- or, in logs, multiplicatively --- into trend, cycle, seasonal
and irregular components,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bsts}
  y_t \;=\; \mu_t \;+\; c_t \;+\; s_t \;+\; e_t,
  \qquad e_t \sim \mathrm{NID}\!\left(0, \sigma_e^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_t$ is a stochastic polynomial trend of order two or lower, $c_t$ a
covariance-stationary AR($p$) cycle, $s_t$ a stochastic seasonal factor, and
$e_t$ irregular noise. The published series is the sum of the smoothed trend
and cycle, so that both seasonality and irregular noise are removed. Series
that national sources publish only in seasonally adjusted form are taken as
given unless residual seasonality remains.

Two features of this choice matter to users. First, because the same model is
applied to every country, seasonality is treated consistently across the
panel, rather than inheriting whatever procedure each national agency
happens to use --- X-12, TRAMO/SEATS, or none. Data not seasonally adjusted at
source are therefore preferred. Second, the same fitted model supplies the
backcasts and nowcasts described above, so smoothing and series completion
are internally consistent. \citet{martinezgarcia2014} provides the formal
evaluation of the method.

\paragraph{Rebasing.}
All completed quarterly series are indexed to 2005\,=\,100. Real series are
obtained using each country's own PCE deflator on the same base.

\subsection{Release Schedule and Vintages}
\label{subsec:release}

The database is released four times a year, on a fixed schedule that builds
in a three-month reporting lag, reflecting the fact that a large share of
countries report the necessary data roughly one quarter in arrears
(Table~\ref{tab:release-schedule}).

  \centering
  \caption{IHPD release schedule \citep{mack2011}}
  \label{tab:release-schedule}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    Last quarter included & Release date \\
    \midrule
    Q4 of year $T-1$ & First full week of April, year $T$ \\
    Q1 of year $T$   & First full week of July, year $T$ \\
    Q2 of year $T$   & First full week of October, year $T$ \\
    Q3 of year $T$   & First full week of January, year $T+1$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Earlier vintages of the database are retained by the Dallas Fed for
researchers working in real time, and are reachable programmatically
(Section~\ref{subsec:ihpdr}). This matters because the series are revised:
national sources revise their own data, nowcast values are replaced by
observed ones, and seasonal adjustment is re-estimated over the full sample
at each release. A given historical quarter can therefore take slightly
different values in different vintages.

\subsection{Data Quality and Known Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The database is designed for cross-country comparability, and the
construction steps above are what deliver it. Each, however, carries a cost,
and the following should be kept in mind.

\paragraph{The panel is balanced by construction, not by observation.}
Every country series spans the full sample without gaps. That completeness is
the product of splicing, interpolation and backcasting, not of every country
having reported comparable quarterly data since 1975. Early observations are
in many cases derived from lower-frequency historical sources, and in a few
cases are model-based backcasts. Users should treat the early part of the
sample as reconstructed, and should not read the absence of missing values as
evidence of uniform data quality across the panel.

\paragraph{Interpolation induces serial correlation.}
Quadratic-match interpolation relates adjacent observations by construction.
\citet{mack2011} note explicitly that this introduces a systematic source of
serial correlation, and that inference using interpolated series should use
standard errors robust to autocorrelation. Averaging monthly data to
quarterly frequency can likewise induce spurious first-order serial
correlation in differenced series.

\paragraph{Source methodologies still differ.}
Selection against a common benchmark narrows cross-country heterogeneity but
does not remove it. National indices differ in how, and whether, they control
for quality change; standardising that treatment is beyond the scope of the
database. Comparisons of dynamics are therefore better founded than
comparisons of levels, and the 2005 rebasing means level comparisons are not
available in any case.

\paragraph{Revisions are retrospective.}
Because seasonal adjustment is re-estimated over the whole sample at each
release, a published observation may change in later vintages even when the
underlying national data have not been revised. Analysis that depends on what
was knowable at a point in time should use the archived vintage for that
date rather than the current release.

\paragraph{The most recent quarter may be partly forecast.}
To keep the release timely and complete, missing current observations are
filled with nowcasts. In the current vintage this affects a number of HPI and
PDI series, each flagged on the \texttt{Note} sheet of the release file.
These values are provisional and are replaced as national data arrive.
